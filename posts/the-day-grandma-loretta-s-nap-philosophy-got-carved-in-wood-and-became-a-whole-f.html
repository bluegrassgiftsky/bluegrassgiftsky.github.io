The Day Grandma Loretta's Nap Philosophy Got Carved in Wood and Became a Whole Family Crisis

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

My grandmother Loretta had a saying for everything. She had one about rain and one about roosters and at least three about people who showed up uninvited at suppertime. But her most-quoted, most-beloved, most-argued-about piece of wisdom was this: 'You never know if a nap is going to be fifteen minutes or four hours, and that's just the Lord keeping you humble.' She said it every single Sunday after the dishes were done. She said it like a benediction. She said it with her eyes already closing.

We grandkids grew up thinking this was just Grandma Loretta being Grandma Loretta. It wasn't until I was a full-grown adult, face-down on my own couch at 2 p.m. on a Saturday, that I understood she had been describing a fundamental truth of human existence. You lie down. You close your eyes. You might wake up refreshed in twenty minutes or you might surface at dusk, disoriented, unsure what year it is, mildly convinced you've missed something important. There is no predicting it. It is, as she would say, a gamble.

So when my cousin Delia texted the family group chat a photo of a carved wood sign that said something along the lines of 'Nap Roulette,' I want you to understand the chaos that followed. Seventeen people. Three generations. One group chat that had previously been used exclusively to coordinate Easter logistics. Within four minutes somebody had typed 'THAT IS GRANDMA LORETTA' in all caps and within six minutes my uncle Harold had somehow brought up a 1987 argument about a card game that I was not alive to witness. I looked up that funny nap decor sign later and just sat there thinking about how a stranger in Kentucky had accidentally summarized my grandmother's entire life philosophy and turned it into wall art.

The thing about a saying like that is it only works if you've earned it. Grandma Loretta wasn't lazy a day in her life. She raised five children, kept a real garden — not a cute garden, a working garden — canned things, sewed things, fixed things. She napped because she'd done enough by noon to justify it. That's the part people miss when they romanticize the slow life. The slow parts were slow because the fast parts were genuinely fast. You don't get to enjoy a Sunday afternoon couch nap the way she did unless you've already outworked half the county before lunch.

It made me think about what we actually put on our walls and why. Most of us don't give it much thought. We hang something because it was on sale or because it fills a space or because someone gave it to us and we felt guilty not displaying it. But the stuff that actually means something, the stuff you notice every day without realizing you're noticing it, is usually tied to a person or a memory. I've seen that same principle in the kind of carved farmhouse wall decor that's been showing up in people's homes lately — not the mass-produced stuff that looks like wood but isn't, but actual solid hardwood with the grain still doing its thing and the letters cut deep enough to catch shadow. There's a realness to it that printed signs just don't have.

Grandma Loretta passed eight years ago and I still think about her nap philosophy more than I think about most things people have told me with the explicit intention of teaching me something. She wasn't trying to be wise. She was just tired and honest about it. The best sayings are usually like that. They're not crafted. They just slip out of somebody's mouth at the right moment and then they stick around for fifty years, outliving the person who said them, ending up in group chats and, apparently, carved into wood in Kentucky workshops.

Delia did end up ordering that sign, by the way. It's hanging in her bedroom now. Every time I visit I stop and look at it and hear Loretta's voice, completely unbidden, saying it one more time. Fifteen minutes or four hours. The Lord keeping you humble. Rest in peace, Grandma. You were right about everything.
