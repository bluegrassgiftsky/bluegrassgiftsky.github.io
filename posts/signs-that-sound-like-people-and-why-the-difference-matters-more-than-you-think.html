Signs That Sound Like People (And Why the Difference Matters More Than You Think)

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

My grandmother had a sign above her kitchen sink. It didn't say KITCHEN. It didn't say GATHER or BLESS THIS MESS or any of the seventeen things you see stacked four-deep at every home goods store between here and Nashville. It said, in her own handwriting, transferred onto a piece of dark walnut by my grandfather sometime in the 1970s: No Crying Over Spilled Milk. But Crying Over Spilled Coffee Is Completely Acceptable. That's it. That's the whole sign. And I have thought about that sign more times than I can count, because it sounded exactly like her.

That's the thing nobody talks about when they talk about home decor. We spend a lot of energy asking whether something looks right -- the right finish, the right font, the right rustic-versus-modern vibe -- and almost no energy asking whether it sounds right. Whether it sounds like the people who actually live in the house.

A sign that reads PANTRY in block letters is not wrong, exactly. It is extremely correct, in fact. It will never embarrass you. It coordinates with everything. It is the decor equivalent of a beige cardigan, and I say that with real affection for beige cardigans. But a sign that reads something like what my grandmother had -- something that has the fingerprints of a real person all over it -- that one does something different. It becomes part of the story of a place instead of just a label for it.

I think about this every time I walk into a kitchen and see a row of those perfectly matched labels. Flour. Sugar. Coffee. Pantry. And I wonder: who chose these words? Did anyone argue about whether Pantry should come before Coffee, or was it just ordered as a set and arranged alphabetically by default? There is nothing wrong with that. But there's also nothing of anyone in it. It could be anyone's kitchen. It is, in a sense, everyone's kitchen.

Compare that to the kitchens that feel like they belong to somebody specific. A hand-lettered chalkboard with a running joke only the family gets. A piece of reclaimed wood with a town name that means something -- not just any town, but the one where someone's people are from. Or even something as simple as a carved wooden pantry sign that has been given a name instead of a category, because in this particular house, the pantry has always been called The Snack Situation, and that is simply its name now.

The difference between those two kitchens is not budget or taste or how much time someone spent on Pinterest. The difference is specificity. The willingness to let a thing be yours instead of generic. And the good news -- the genuinely freeing news -- is that specificity is not expensive. It's not even hard. It mostly just requires deciding that your house is allowed to sound like you.

Somewhere between the all-caps GATHER signs and the heirloom walnut piece your grandfather made is a whole range of things that are meaningful without being precious, personal without being inside jokes nobody else gets. A small workshop out of Kentucky that I ran across a while back -- you can read a little about how they got started on their our story page -- makes carved hardwood signs the old-fashioned way, which is to say the wood is actually carved, not printed, not vinyl, not sticker-over-plywood. The difference in how that looks and feels in person is genuinely surprising if you haven't seen it before.

None of which changes the real point, which is that the object is only as good as the words on it. A beautifully carved sign with a catalog phrase is still a catalog phrase. A rough-cut piece of pine with the right words is a family heirloom in twenty years. My grandmother's sign wasn't fancy. It was just hers. That's the whole trick. Let the thing sound like someone actually lives there.
