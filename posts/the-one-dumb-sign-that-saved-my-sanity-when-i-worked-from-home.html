The One Dumb Sign That Saved My Sanity When I Worked From Home

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

Let me tell you about the lowest point of my remote-work era. It was a Tuesday — and I only knew it was Tuesday because I'd written the word TUESDAY on a Post-it note and stuck it to my monitor, which tells you everything about my grip on reality at that point. I was living in the same fourteen-by-twelve room where I slept, ate lunch, attended meetings in a blazer-and-pajama-pants combo, and occasionally just sat staring at the wall like a golden retriever who forgot what he was barking at. Something had to change.

I couldn't repaint. Couldn't knock out walls. Couldn't afford the standing desk that every productivity influencer was hawking like it was the cure for the common soul. What I could do, apparently, was hang things on walls, because the lease said nothing about nails under a certain size and I was going to exploit that loophole to its absolute limit.

So I started putting up signs. Not the inspirational kind — Lord, not those. Nothing against people who wake up energized by the phrase 'Hustle Harder' in shiplap font, but that's not my nervous system. I wanted something that felt like a person actually lived and worked here. Something with a little personality. A little grit.

The first one I hung was a simple carved wooden piece that just said the name of the room's function, basically my own little office door plate, except the door didn't exist and the office was also my dining room. Didn't matter. The moment it went up on the wall, something shifted. The space felt declared. Official. Like I had staked a flag in this fourteen-by-twelve patch of carpet and said: this is where the work happens now, and we're going to treat it like that.

I know how that sounds. It's a piece of wood with letters in it. I'm not claiming it cured anything. But there is something genuinely underrated about giving a space a name, a visual anchor, a thing on the wall that says this place means something. Interior designers charge a lot of money to tell you exactly this, and I accidentally stumbled into it via a carved sign that cost less than my last sad sad desk lamp.

The second sign I hung — and this is the one that really did the work — was a gift I'd originally bought for my mom as a joke. It's the kind of thing you find when you go looking for a present for a woman who is impossible to shop for, deeply practical, and has a sense of humor that runs about fifty percent dry wit and fifty percent barely-contained chaos. I ended up keeping it for myself after I caught myself laughing at it every single morning, which, during that period of my life, was not nothing. If you've got a mom cut from similar cloth, that carved funny sign for the mom who holds everything together might be the most honest gift you give this year.

Anyway. The point isn't really the signs. The point is that remote work quietly erodes the line between where you are and what you're doing, and you have to actively draw that line back, every day, in whatever small ridiculous ways are available to you. For some people it's a dedicated coffee cup. For some it's a commute they fake by walking around the block. For me it was apparently hardwood and a router bit. Whatever works.

By the time I went back to an office — a real one, with a parking lot and fluorescent lights and a break room that smelled faintly of someone's reheated salmon — I actually felt a little sad leaving my cobbled-together home setup. The signs stayed up for months after. One of them is still there. A little testament to the year I figured out that a space doesn't become yours because you spend time in it. It becomes yours because you decide it is. Preferably in writing. Preferably in carved oak.

If you're curious what that kind of thing looks like, the folks at this small Kentucky workshop do it right — solid wood, hand-carved, no vinyl nonsense. Worth a look if you're furnishing a home office or shopping for someone who deserves something with a little more soul than a gift card.
