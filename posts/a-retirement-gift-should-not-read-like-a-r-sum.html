A Retirement Gift Should Not Read Like a Résumé

Every October the inbox shifts. Through the summer it's weddings and new houses. Then the first week of October hits and I start getting emails from office managers who have been handed a budget and a deadline, because somebody is leaving on December 31 and the party is the week before.

These emails almost always contain the same four things: the person's full name, their job title, their hire date, and the number of years they put in. Sometimes a department name. Sometimes a line like "In recognition of 32 years of dedicated service."

I carve it if that's what they want. It's their gift and their money. But I want to make the case, politely and with a straight face, that you are about to hand someone a document, and documents belong in a file.

The number of years is an HR metric

Thirty-two years is a real thing. It happened. But that number was generated by a payroll system, and the person it describes is probably the only one in the room who already knows it by heart. Putting it on a wall sign tells them something they lived through, in the voice of the place they just left.

Here's the test I use. Imagine the sign hanging in their house five years from now, in the hallway between the bathroom and the back bedroom. Does it still do anything? "In recognition of 32 years of dedicated service" is a thing that stops working the day after the party, because the recognition has already happened. The party was the recognition.

What keeps working is something that's theirs. A phrase they said so often that everybody in the building can do the impression. The nickname for the thing they built or fixed or refused to let die. The name of the lake house, the boat, the farm, the shop they're finally going to spend time in. A last name and a year, if you want something simple that reads like a cornerstone instead of a performance review.

There's a practical reason too, and it's about letters

Our signs are CNC-carved on one face, out of solid pine or poplar, then finished by hand here in Kentucky. That setup has opinions built into it.

Carving is subtractive. Every letter is a V-shaped trench cut into the board, and the deeper and wider that trench is, the more shadow it throws, and shadow is what you're actually reading from across a room. When you hand me a long sentence, the letters have to shrink to fit the face. Small letters get shallow cuts. Shallow cuts throw a thin shadow. You end up with a board that looks great in your hand and goes mushy from eight feet away.

Pine and poplar behave a little differently about this. Pine has soft early wood and harder late wood in alternating bands, so fine serifs can get a touch fuzzy where the bit crosses from one to the other, and we clean that up by hand. Poplar is more even-tempered and holds tighter detail. Either way, the fix for most legibility problems isn't a different wood or a different bit. It's fewer words.

So the two arguments land in the same place. Short is better because short is more personal, and short is better because short carves deeper and reads farther. The job title and the service years are usually what's eating the space.

What I'd put on it instead

  • A phrase, not a sentence. Something they say. Four or five words is plenty.
  • A place. Where they're going, not where they've been. Lake names and farm names carve beautifully.
  • A date that's theirs. Not the hire date. The last day, if they're proud of it, or nothing at all.
  • Their actual name. Not "Mr." and not the title. The name their family uses.

The group-gift problem

Offices tend to want one object that forty people can sign a card for. Fine. But think about where each kind of thing lives. A carved sign hangs on a wall and gets looked at. Printed items — mugs, shirts, glasses, flasks, the designs drawn up here and printed to order by our partner — live in cabinets and drawers and get used, which is its own kind of good. Those are the ones where an inside joke actually earns its keep, because the only people seeing it are the ones who get it.

And if you need something for them to open at the party itself, hand-poured wax melts are an easy, unfussy add. Nobody has to make a speech about a wax melt.

One last thing, since it's October and you've got time. Ask one person who knows them well what the retiree is actually going to do in January. Then put that on the board. It's a better gift, it carves cleaner, and it doesn't read like a form.
