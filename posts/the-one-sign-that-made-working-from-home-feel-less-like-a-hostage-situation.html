The One Sign That Made Working From Home Feel Less Like a Hostage Situation

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

Let me paint you a picture. It is March 2020-something, I am on my fourth consecutive hour of video calls, and my husband has wandered into the background of at least two of them holding a bowl of cereal he absolutely did not need at two in the afternoon. We are both working from home now, indefinitely, in a house that was designed for people who occasionally see each other at dinner — not two full-time professionals who are suddenly coworkers, roommates, and the entire social life each other has got.

Nobody warns you about this part. The articles were all about ergonomic chairs and standing desks and finding your flow. Nobody said: hey, you are about to learn exactly how your spouse loads the dishwasher, and you are going to have Opinions about it, and those Opinions are going to live in your chest like a small angry bird for the next eighteen months.

We got through it, obviously. Most couples did. But I will tell you the thing that helped more than any productivity hack or couples communication worksheet — and I have done both, no shame — was leaning into the absurdity of it. Stopping trying to pretend we were two professional adults with boundaries and a commute, and just admitting that we were two people deeply in each other's business, eating each other's snacks, and somehow still in love despite it.

The physical evidence of this surrender lives on our office wall. It is a solid wood sign, hand-carved, that reads: love is pretending not to see each other eat that extra slice of cake. I found that funny couple decor piece when I was looking for something to hang in the room we'd started calling the office even though it is also where we keep the cat's litter box and three boxes we have not unpacked since we moved in four years ago. It made me laugh out loud alone at my desk, which was the whole criterion. It now hangs directly in my sightline during calls, and it has saved approximately eleven conversations that were about to become arguments.

There is something about naming the thing, you know? When you put it on the wall in carved wood — not a printed poster, not a vinyl sticker, actual wood with actual texture — it becomes a little bit official. Like you have signed a treaty. We, the undersigned parties, agree that we saw each other eat that cake and we are choosing not to make it weird.

My husband, for his part, added his own contribution to the decor a few months later. He found a sign that says my wife isn't fragile like a flower, she's fragile like a bomb. He claims this is a compliment. I have chosen, in the spirit of the treaty, to believe him. That bold wife wall art is also carved hardwood, which means it is going to outlast both of us and probably the house, which feels like appropriate stakes for the sentiment.

Here is what I actually want to tell you, though, if you have made it this far without owning a small business or a wood shop or any personal stake in home decor: the things you put on your walls matter more than we act like they do. Not in a woo-woo way. Just in a plain human way where you look at your surroundings thirty or forty times a day and they are either saying something true about who you are, or they are not. A motivational quote you do not believe is just noise. But something that makes you snort-laugh on a bad Tuesday? That is load-bearing decor. That is infrastructure.

We still work from home. The commute is still zero minutes. My husband still eats cereal at inadvisable hours. But we have a wall that basically says: yeah, we see each other, all of it, and we are still here. Turns out that is more than enough.
