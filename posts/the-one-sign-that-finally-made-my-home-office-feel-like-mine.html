The One Sign That Finally Made My Home Office Feel Like Mine

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

I want to tell you about the worst year of my professional life, which also happened to be the year I accidentally became a person who cares deeply about wall decor. These two facts are not unrelated.

When the whole work-from-home thing went from temporary inconvenience to permanent lifestyle, I did what most people did: I set up shop in whatever room had a door. For me that was the spare bedroom, which had previously housed a treadmill I never used, a box of holiday cards I never sent, and approximately four hundred wire hangers with no apparent origin story. Real inspiring stuff.

The first six months I told myself it was fine. It was not fine. I was spending eight to ten hours a day in a room that communicated, loudly and clearly, that I was a person who had given up. The treadmill judged me. The wire hangers rattled in a draft that should not have existed. And my video call backgrounds looked like the before photo in a storage unit intervention show.

So I started paying attention to spaces that felt good. Not in a Pinterest-spiral way — more like noticing what made certain rooms feel like somewhere rather than nowhere. And the thing I kept coming back to was: personality. Specific, committed, slightly ridiculous personality. A room that has a point of view. A room that has, at minimum, one object in it that says something about who lives there.

For me, that object turned out to be a sign. Not a printed-on-canvas motivational quote with a cursive font and a faux-distressed edge. I am done with those. I mean something with actual weight to it — wood that you can feel the grain of, letters that were carved into the surface rather than applied on top of it like a sticker pretending to be furniture.

I found a small Kentucky workshop that does exactly that kind of work, and I spent an embarrassing amount of time reading about how they got started — the kind of small-family-origin story that makes you feel like your money is going somewhere real. Their whole approach is that a sign should feel like it belongs to a place, not just be dropped into one.

I ended up ordering something for my office that I genuinely will not repeat here because my mother reads this blog. But I will say it involved a pun, it involved my professional title, and it now hangs directly behind my monitor during every video call. Three people have asked where I got it. One of them was my boss. That meeting went fine.

The other thing I bought — and this one I can mention — was for the downstairs bathroom. We have exactly one bathroom on the main floor of our house, and it is approximately the size of a generous coat closet, and during any family gathering it becomes a bottleneck situation that I find genuinely stressful. I needed something on that door that communicated, warmly but firmly, the correct expectations. I found this outhouse-style carved bathroom sign, which reads Shit and Split, and it has done more for family gathering logistics than any conversation I have ever tried to have. It is funny. It is direct. It is carved from real wood. It has earned its place.

The point I keep coming back to — the thing I genuinely think about now when I look at any room — is that a space starts to feel livable the moment it contains something that was put there on purpose. Not to fill a gap. Not because it was on sale. Because someone made a choice. That choice, in my office, was a sign with a dumb pun on it that makes me smile every single time I sit down to work, which has turned out to be worth more than I would have predicted back in the wire-hanger days.

Start with one thing. Make it specific. Make it yours. The treadmill, by the way, is gone. Sold it. No regrets.
