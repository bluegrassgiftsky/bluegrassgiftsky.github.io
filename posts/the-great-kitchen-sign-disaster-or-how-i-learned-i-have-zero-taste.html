The Great Kitchen Sign Disaster (Or: How I Learned I Have Zero Taste)

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

I want to tell you about the six weeks I spent trying to pick out a sign for my kitchen, because I think it will make you feel better about your own decision-making abilities. Whatever choices you have made in your life -- whatever you regret, whatever keeps you up at night -- you did not spend six weeks agonizing over a piece of wood that says something about coffee. That is my cross to bear, and I carry it with dignity.

It started innocently enough. My kitchen had this one bare wall above the breakfast nook, about eighteen inches of nothing between the cabinet and the window trim, and my mother-in-law visited in October and said, and I quote, 'You could really do something with that space.' Now, my mother-in-law is a lovely woman, but those seven words activated something in my brain that I cannot fully explain. I became convinced that the correct sign in that exact spot would transform my entire kitchen into a place where a person could be happy.

First I went through the coffee phase. Every kitchen sign in the universe, I am fairly certain, is about coffee. 'But First, Coffee.' 'Life Begins After Coffee.' 'Coffee Is My Love Language.' I am not opposed to coffee -- I drink it every morning with the enthusiasm of someone defusing a bomb -- but I did not want to make a permanent declaration about it. What if I go through a tea phase? What if I develop a sensitivity? I am not putting my identity in the hands of a beverage.

Then I pivoted to the gathering-place angle. You know the ones. 'This Kitchen Is Seasoned With Love.' 'Where Family Comes Together.' 'Good Food, Good Friends, Good Life.' And look, I believe in all of those sentiments in the abstract. But something about putting them on my wall made me feel like I was lying. My kitchen is where I burn toast and argue with my husband about whether the dishwasher is full enough to run. It is not a Williams-Sonoma catalog. I owe it the truth.

I briefly considered something funny. A sign that said 'I Only Have A Kitchen Because It Came With The House,' which, relatable, but also my mother-in-law was going to see this thing and I did not need that conversation. I thought about something with our family name on it, which felt dignified, but then I realized I was going to have to look at my own last name every single morning before I had coffee, and that seemed like a lot.

My husband, bless his heart, stopped weighing in around week three. He had opinions in week one -- strong ones, even -- but by week four he would just walk through the kitchen, glance at the bare wall, and keep moving with the expression of a man who has made his peace with uncertainty. He is a good man. He deserves better than what I put him through.

Here is what I eventually figured out, and this is the part that might actually be useful to somebody: the sign was never the problem. The bare wall was not the problem either. I was trying to use a piece of decor to answer a question about what kind of home I wanted to have, and that is too much weight to put on any object, wooden or otherwise. A sign can reflect who you are. It cannot tell you.

I ended up going with something simple. Our last name, clean lettering, carved into a nice piece of walnut. No declarations, no philosophy, no promises about coffee. Just the name of the people who live here, which felt honest. My mother-in-law saw it and said it was 'understated,' which in her vocabulary lands somewhere between 'I don't hate it' and 'I would have done something different,' and honestly, that is about all I was going for. The wall is no longer bare. The kitchen is still where I burn toast. Everything is as it should be.
