The Day Grandma Edna's Words Became Permanent

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

My grandmother Edna had a saying for everything. Burned the biscuits? 'Well, we're livin' our best life.' Ran out of sweet tea at the reunion? 'We're livin' our best life.' Car broke down on the way to church in August, in Kentucky, in a polyester dress? You already know. She said it so often and with such complete sincerity that it stopped being funny and started being philosophy. The woman could have charged for therapy. She did not.

She passed in 2019, and here's the thing nobody tells you about grief: the phrases go quiet before you realize they were holding the house together. You don't notice until you burn something on the stove and the kitchen just sits there, silent, not offering any wisdom whatsoever. That's when you understand that some people's words were doing structural work.

My cousin Patrice is the one who finally did something about it. She'd been poking around online looking for something to hang in her kitchen -- not a farmhouse stencil situation, not a vinyl decal that peels at the corners after one humid August, but something that actually had some weight to it. She stumbled across a carved hardwood sign, the kind routed into solid wood so the letters have actual shadow and depth, and it said Living Our Best Life. She sent me a photo with zero context. Just the image. I stared at it for a solid thirty seconds.

I called her immediately. 'Patrice.' Long pause. 'I know,' she said.

She ordered it. It now lives above the stove in her kitchen in Lexington, and I will not pretend I didn't tear up a little the first time I saw it hanging there. It looks like it was always supposed to be there. That's the thing about wood -- it doesn't look like a product someone purchased. It looks like a decision someone made. There's a difference.

The whole episode sent me down a rabbit hole, because of course it did. Turns out there's a whole corner of the internet dedicated to the specific art of putting words on wood in ways that don't make your house look like a joke gift shop. I found a blog post about funny mom signs she'll actually hang on her wall that had me laughing out loud at my kitchen table, partly because several of them sounded exactly like things Edna would have said -- or things she'd have said about her own mother. Some sayings are just load-bearing across generations.

What I didn't expect from that rabbit hole was to get a little sentimental about craft. CNC carving -- which is what makes these signs look so clean and dimensional -- is a genuinely interesting process. A computer guides the router bit, but somebody still has to design the lettering, choose the wood, decide how deep to cut, how to finish it. There's real decision-making involved, even when a machine is doing the cutting. The result is something that photographs beautifully but feels even better in person, because the texture is actually there. Your finger finds the grooves. That matters more than it sounds like it should.

Patrice and I have talked since then about what other phrases of Edna's deserve to be permanent. She had about forty-seven good ones. 'A little rain never stopped a good hat' is a contender. So is 'You can be right or you can be happy, and some days you can be both.' We're taking our time. Some words earn the wood.

If you've got a grandmother's phrase living rent-free in your head -- or a mom who dispenses wisdom like she's getting paid by the proverb -- you probably already know what I mean. Some things are worth making permanent. And some sayings are just too good to let rattle around inside one person's memory forever.
