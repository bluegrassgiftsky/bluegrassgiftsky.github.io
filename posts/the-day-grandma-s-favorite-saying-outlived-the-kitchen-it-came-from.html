The Day Grandma's Favorite Saying Outlived the Kitchen It Came From

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

My grandmother had a saying for everything. Burn the biscuits? She'd shrug and say the butter hides a multitude of sins. Rain on a picnic? The good Lord's just washing the tablecloth. But her absolute favorite, the one she deployed at least twice a week whether it applied or not, was this: teamwork makes the dream work. She'd say it when she and my grandfather were canning tomatoes together. She'd say it when two of us kids carried in the grocery bags at the same time. She said it once, memorably, when a barn cat and a hound dog cornered the same mouse from opposite ends of the feed room. That cat and dog were not a team. But Grandma called it anyway.

She passed about six years ago, and I won't pretend the grief was tidy or short. What surprised me was what I missed most. Not the big things — not the holidays or the recipes, though lord knows I miss those too. It was the sayings. The specific, slightly-too-cheerful, grammatically optional little phrases she'd lob into the room like a verbal casserole. The house got quiet in a way that had nothing to do with sound.

My cousin Della and I were cleaning out the farmhouse the spring after, which is a job I would not wish on anyone who hasn't first stretched emotionally and hydrated properly. We found ourselves in the kitchen at about two in the afternoon, surrounded by cast iron and mason jars and forty years of calendars Grandma had kept because she liked the pictures. Della looked at me across a box of doilies and said, completely unprompted, teamwork makes the dream work. We both laughed until we cried, and then cried until we laughed, and then made a pot of coffee and didn't move for an hour.

That was the moment I decided the saying needed to go somewhere permanent. Not in a scrapbook, not in a notes app, not cross-stitched onto a pillow that would spend fifty years in a closet. I wanted it carved. Actually carved, the way things used to be made — with depth you could run your finger across, wood grain that does something different in the afternoon light than it does in the morning. I'd been looking around for options and stumbled onto a Kentucky workshop doing exactly that, real CNC-carved hardwood, and they had a carved wood office sign with that very phrase already in their catalog, which felt a little like the universe being smug about something.

The sign ended up in Della's home office. She works remotely now, managing a team of people spread across three time zones, and she says sometimes on a bad call she just looks up at it and breathes. Grandma would have loved that. She also would have had a joke about conference calls that I am not in a position to reconstruct from memory, but it would have been good.

Here is the part of the story that surprises people: the sign made me want to look at the rest of what that shop makes. I wasn't expecting to fall a little bit in love with a cat-themed piece, but then again, I also wasn't expecting to spend an hour weeping over a box of doilies, so clearly my expectations need recalibrating. They have this caffeinated whiskers sign that is so specifically right for a certain kind of person — the kind of person who has named all their cats after literary figures and considers a second cup of coffee a personality trait — that I immediately thought of three different people I know. Two of them got it for Christmas.

The whole experience reminded me of something I'd half-forgotten: that the things worth keeping are rarely the things we plan to keep. Nobody sits around thinking, someday I will treasure this goofy saying my grandmother repeated into the void every time two people did anything in the same room. And then you do. You treasure it enormously. You put it on hardwood. You ship it to your cousin who is managing people across time zones and needs something to look at on a hard Tuesday.

Grandma would have called that teamwork. She really would have.
