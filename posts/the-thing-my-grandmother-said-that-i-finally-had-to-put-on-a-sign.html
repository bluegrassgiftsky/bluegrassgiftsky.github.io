The Thing My Grandmother Said That I Finally Had to Put on a Sign

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

My grandmother had a saying for everything. Burn the biscuits? She had a saying. Car wouldn't start in January? Saying. Somebody showed up uninvited during the church playoffs on television? Oh, she had a very specific saying for that one, and it involved the phrase 'bless your heart' in a tone that meant approximately the opposite of what it sounds like. The woman was a walking almanac of Southern wisdom, most of it delivered while stirring something on the stove without looking up.

But the one I remember most wasn't poetic. It wasn't stitched on a pillow or printed on a church bulletin. It was just what she said every single time somebody new walked through her front door, usually before they'd even gotten their coat off: 'Hope you like dog hair, because it's included free of charge.' She had two beagles named Biscuit and Trouble — named appropriately, I promise you — and she was not the least bit apologetic about them or their contribution to every fabric surface in the house.

I thought about that saying for years after she passed. It's funny how grief works. You don't always miss the big things first. Sometimes it's the throwaway lines, the stuff she said so often it became wallpaper, that suddenly feels irreplaceable. I found myself saying it to my own guests, about my own ridiculous dog, and every time I did, I felt her in the room a little bit.

So last winter I decided to do something about it. I'd been down a rabbit hole of home decor — as one does at eleven-thirty on a Wednesday when the dog is snoring on the couch and there's nothing good on television — and I stumbled onto some carved wooden signs. Not the printed kind. Not vinyl sticker lettering. Actual carved hardwood, letters routed right into the wood, the kind that looks like somebody made it with their hands and their time. And I found this funny dog hair wooden sign that stopped me cold. There it was. My grandmother's whole philosophy, rendered in solid wood.

I ordered it. It arrived. I hung it right by the front door, which is exactly where she would have put it, and I stood there in my hallway and laughed until I had to sit down on the floor. The dog came over to investigate, which felt appropriate.

Here's the thing I didn't expect: other people's reactions. Guests walk in, they read it, and before I can even say hello, they're telling me about their grandmother. Or their neighbor's cat. Or the dog they had growing up. That little carved sign has started more conversations than anything else in my house, including the questionable abstract painting I bought at an estate sale that everyone politely pretends not to notice.

It got me thinking about all the sayings we let disappear. The kitchen-table wisdom, the doorstep jokes, the things our people said so often they stopped registering as anything special — right up until they were gone. There's something about seeing words carved into something solid that makes them feel permanent in a way that matters. Like you're saying: this one, we're keeping.

I've since poked around more and noticed they also make a carved welcome sign for cat hair households, for anybody whose grandmother ran a different kind of operation. Because honestly, the philosophy is universal. The hair just varies. Whatever your household produces in abundance — dog hair, cat hair, questionable decisions, very strong opinions about college football — there's probably a sign for it. And if there isn't, there should be.

Grandmother, wherever you are: Biscuit and Trouble would have approved. The sign is crooked by about four degrees because I hung it myself and I am my mother's child, but the words are right. That's what you always said mattered most.
