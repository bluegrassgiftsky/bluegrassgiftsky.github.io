The Sign That Makes Sense to Exactly Six People on Earth

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

Every family has one. A joke so deeply embedded in the lore of your particular group of weirdos that you cannot explain it to an outsider without watching their eyes go polite and distant. You see the exact moment they decide to just smile and nod. And honestly, that smile-and-nod is its own reward, because it means the joke still belongs only to you.

For my family, the joke is the phrase Bless Your Potatoes. I am not going to explain where it came from. Partly because it would take forty-five minutes and require a diagram, and partly because the whole point of a family inside joke is that it resists explanation. What I will tell you is that it started at a Thanksgiving somewhere in the mid-2000s, involved my Aunt Renee saying something genuinely profound about casserole, and has since been deployed at every family gathering as a greeting, a farewell, a toast, a warning, and at least once as a threat. It means everything. It means nothing. It is ours.

The problem with inside jokes is that they live in the air. They exist in the moment someone says the thing and everyone else loses it while the non-family spouse in the corner stares into their sweet tea wondering what they married into. That is a beautiful, fragile kind of thing. But it does not hang on a wall. It does not greet you when you walk into the kitchen on a Tuesday morning when nobody else is around and you just need a little reminder that your people are your people.

So last year my cousin Deb, who is the most sentimental of all of us and owns at least four crocheted throw pillows that say things, commissioned a wooden sign. Not a printed thing. Not a vinyl decal on a piece of pine that will peel in three years. An actually carved sign, routed right into solid hardwood so the letters have that shadow and weight that make words feel like they mean something. She found a little Kentucky workshop that does exactly that kind of work, and I will drop a mention here because I have since fallen down that particular rabbit hole myself: they have a rustic wooden memory quote sign that is genuinely lovely if you have a phrase worth keeping, which, if you are reading this, you probably do.

The sign arrived and Deb put it up in her kitchen. And here is the thing about a carved wooden sign that says something only six people understand: it works better than any sign that says something everyone understands. A stranger walks in and sees Bless Your Potatoes in solid walnut above the window and they tilt their head. They think maybe it is a regional expression they missed. Maybe it is scripture. Maybe Deb just really loves potatoes. They do not ask because it seems like maybe they should already know. And Deb does not explain. She just smiles.

There is actually something worth chewing on here about why kitchens specifically collect this kind of meaning. The kitchen is where families negotiate who they are. It is where the jokes get born, where the recipes get passed down wrong and then corrected and then passed down wrong again on purpose because the wrong version is better. I have been thinking about this since I read a piece about wood-carved charm in Kentucky kitchens that articulated it better than I could, which is the whole reason I am still thinking about it weeks later.

The moral of this story, if there is one, is go ahead and make the sign. Make the sign that will confuse your future son-in-law. Make the sign that references the thing that happened at the lake in 2009. Make the sign nobody outside your zip code will understand. The people who need to understand it will walk in, read it, and feel exactly the way you want them to feel. Everyone else will just assume it is charming, and they will be right, just not for the reason they think.

Bless your potatoes, friends. You know who you are.
