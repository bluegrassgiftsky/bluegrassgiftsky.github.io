Your Sign Says Welcome. Does It Sound Like You?

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

My grandmother had a sign above her back door that said BLESS THIS MESS. It was crooked. The paint had started to peel somewhere around 1987 and nobody fixed it because fixing it would have been beside the point. That sign was not decorating her kitchen. That sign was her kitchen. Every casserole dish left on the counter, every grandkid's drawing stuck to the fridge with a magnet shaped like a pineapple, every argument about whose turn it was to dry -- that crooked little sign presided over all of it like a tiny wooden judge who had already ruled in everyone's favor.

I thought about that sign recently when I was trying to help a friend pick out something for her new house. We spent about forty-five minutes looking at options online, and I started to notice something. Almost everything fell into one of two categories. There were signs that told you facts -- KITCHEN, LAUNDRY, FARMHOUSE, HOME -- like little wooden name tags for rooms that already knew what they were. And then there were signs that tried so hard to be charming that they swung clean past charming and landed somewhere in the gift-shop-at-a-highway-rest-stop district. Live. Laugh. Love. Gather. Blessed.

Nothing wrong with any of those words. The trouble is they don't belong to anybody in particular. They belong to everybody in general, which is pretty close to the same thing as belonging to nobody. You could swap one of those signs between a thousand different homes and not a single resident would notice the difference. That's the catalog problem. Catalog language is optimized to offend no one and therefore to move no one either.

So what makes a sign actually sound like a family? I've been turning this over and I think it comes down to something embarrassingly simple: specificity. The more specific a thing is, the more it belongs to someone real. My grandmother's BLESS THIS MESS worked because it was true. Her house was, in fact, a mess. The sign wasn't aspirational. It wasn't telling you what she wished she was. It was telling you exactly what she was, and she thought that was worth celebrating.

The best versions of these things -- the ones that make you stop and actually look -- tend to have a little friction in them. They reference an inside joke nobody outside the family gets. They use a nickname. They have a date that seems random but obviously isn't. They quote something the grandpa actually said at Thanksgiving once, something that became family legend. They're a little weird. They maybe don't even make complete grammatical sense, but they make complete emotional sense to the people who live there.

Here's a useful test I made up just now and am very proud of: if you could imagine the same sign in a hotel lobby, it probably belongs in a hotel lobby. Hotels need words like WELCOME and REST and GATHER because they're trying to appeal to every possible human being who might walk through the door. Your house is trying to do the opposite. Your house is supposed to repel most of humanity and deeply comfort the specific eight or twelve people who are yours.

The other thing worth saying is that the medium matters more than people think. There's a reason my grandmother's sign was painted wood and not a printed poster. Wood has weight. It has grain. It has a little imperfection built into the material itself. When you put words into something that took effort and left a mark, those words carry more gravity than the same words printed on paper or stuck on with vinyl. It's the same reason a handwritten note feels different from a text even when the words are identical. The effort is part of the message.

My friend ended up choosing something that referenced a dumb thing her husband says every single time they leave for vacation. I'm not going to repeat it here because it wouldn't be funny to you -- and that is exactly the point. It was funny to her. It was true. It was theirs. And I promise you, thirty years from now, somebody is going to stand in her kitchen looking at that crooked, paint-peeling sign and feel completely at home.
