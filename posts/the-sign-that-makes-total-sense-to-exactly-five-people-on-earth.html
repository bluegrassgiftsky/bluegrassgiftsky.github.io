The Sign That Makes Total Sense to Exactly Five People on Earth

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

Every family has one. A phrase so specific, so rooted in one chaotic Tuesday fifteen years ago, that it functions like a password. Say it to an outsider and they stare at you like you just sneezed in a foreign language. Say it to your cousin at Thanksgiving and both of you lose it completely while everyone else goes back to eating green bean casserole in polite confusion. Ours is two words: Biscuit Situation.

I won't bore you with the full story — partly because context won't help anyway, and partly because even typing it out, I can feel it deflating in real time. The short version is that my grandmother, at a church potluck circa 2007, made a judgment call about a basket of biscuits that was either heroic or catastrophic depending on who you ask. The phrase entered the family lexicon that same afternoon. Now it means approximately seventeen different things: a mild crisis, a logistical scramble, a moment of unexpected grace, a bad idea that somehow worked. We use it constantly. Nobody outside the family has ever once known what we're talking about.

So naturally, we put it on a sign.

My sister found a little Kentucky woodshop that does hand-carved hardwood pieces — actual CNC-carved solid wood, not printed vinyl that peels off in three winters — and she commissioned a Biscuit Situation sign for our mom's kitchen. She agonized over the font. She sent the shop three separate emails about sizing. She made it a whole thing. The sign arrived, we hung it above the coffee maker, and it has now lived there for two years. Every single person who visits our mother's house asks what it means. Every single time, our mother just smiles and says, I'll explain later, and never does.

This is the highest and best use of a wooden sign. Not inspiration. Not a farmhouse proverb. Not your last name in big block letters over the fireplace. A complete non-sequitur that is secretly the most meaningful sentence in your house.

I've been thinking about this a lot lately because I went down a rabbit hole looking at what people actually put on their walls, and the range is genuinely beautiful. On one end, you've got the earnest stuff — and look, I'm not above it, there's a place for a carved say what you mean sign that just tells you exactly where it stands, no decoration, no hedging, and honestly sometimes that's the energy a room needs. On the other end you've got the fully unhinged, like a weed the people carved wood piece that is either a political statement, a gardening joke, or both, and is probably hanging in a sunroom somewhere being enjoyed by someone who has absolutely figured out their whole vibe.

But the sweet spot, I think, is the family language sign. The phrase that doesn't translate. My friend growing up had a family that said the coast is bumpy whenever someone was being difficult, and to this day I don't know where that came from, and neither do they, and it doesn't matter. If that phrase were carved into a piece of solid wood and hung in their entryway, it would be the most honest thing in the house.

The beautiful thing about inside jokes is that they're not really jokes at all — they're compressed history. A biscuit situation is also: the year we all drove eight hours for a potluck, the grandmother who has since passed, the church hall that smelled like candles and casserole, every ordinary Sunday that turned out to matter. You can't put all of that on a sign. But you can put two words that point at all of it, and hang them where you'll see them every morning while the coffee brews.

Your family has a phrase. You know the one. The question is just whether you're going to keep it rattling around in your heads or put it somewhere it can stay.
