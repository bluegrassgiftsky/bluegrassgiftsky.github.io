The Day Grandma Ruth's Mouth Got Immortalized in Hardwood

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

My grandmother Ruth was a tiny woman who stood five-foot-two in her church shoes and could silence a room full of grown men with a single look. She had a saying for everything. Burned the biscuits? That's character. Kids tracking mud through the kitchen? The floor's seen worse. But her all-time greatest, the one she deployed like a surgical instrument whenever one of us pushed our luck, was delivered with both hands flat on the counter and her reading glasses sliding down her nose: Thou shalt not try me. Mom 24:7. She didn't explain it. She didn't repeat it. You just understood.

She passed in 2019, and for a couple of years after that, the phrase kind of floated around our family conversations the way her things did — a little sacred, a little funny, always hers. My cousin Deanna started saying it to her own teenagers. My mom stitched it into a cross-stitch she never quite finished. I tried to write it on a chalkboard in my kitchen, which lasted about eleven days before someone erased it to write the grocery list.

The problem with chalkboards, magnets, printouts, and cross-stitches is that they're all a little provisional. They look like something you could change your mind about. Grandma Ruth did not deal in provisional. She dealt in permanent.

So last fall, I went looking for something that would actually hold the weight of the thing, and I ended up on a Kentucky woodworking shop's site where I found a carved wood version of that exact saying — that Thou Shalt Not Try Me sign, solid hardwood, CNC-carved so the letters sit in the wood itself rather than sitting on top of it like a sticker pretending to be serious. I cannot explain to you how satisfying it is to find a thing that already exists when you thought you were going to have to describe it to someone from scratch.

I ordered it without overthinking it, which is rare for me. When it arrived, I held it for a minute before I even unwrapped it all the way. The grain ran right through the lettering. The carving had actual depth. It wasn't a picture of a saying — it was the saying, in the wood. I put it in the kitchen, which is where Grandma Ruth spent most of her best years and delivered most of her best lines.

My mom cried a little when she saw it. Then she laughed, because that's what the phrase calls for. Deanna wants one for herself. My uncle, who is seventy-one years old and was on the receiving end of the original phrase more times than any of us, said — and I'm quoting directly — that it looked like something that should have always been on a wall.

There's something worth sitting with in that reaction. We spend a lot of time in our homes surrounded by things that are decorative but weightless — pretty colors, nice textures, vibes. But the stuff that lasts, the stuff people actually stop and look at, tends to carry a specific story or a specific person. A piece of wood with real depth and a real phrase carved into it is doing something a print or a vinyl decal just isn't, and if you want to think about why that is from a design standpoint, there's actually a pretty good piece on how bold, substantial decor changes the feeling of a room entirely — the kind of thinking that applies whether you're hanging something in a kitchen or dressing up a home office.

I'm not here to tell you to go buy a sign. I'm here to tell you that sometimes you find the exact right object for a person who isn't here anymore to hold it, and it does exactly what you needed it to do. Grandma Ruth's mouth, in solid hardwood, on my kitchen wall, telling the world — or at least my kitchen — exactly where the line is. She'd say it was about time.
