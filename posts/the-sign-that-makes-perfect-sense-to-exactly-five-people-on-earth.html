The Sign That Makes Perfect Sense to Exactly Five People on Earth

Notes from the workshop · Bluegrass Gifts

Every family has one. A phrase so specific, so deeply rooted in some chaotic Tuesday from twelve years ago, that saying it out loud in mixed company gets you nothing but polite smiles and the slow backing-away of strangers. My family's phrase is Pickle Protocol. I am not going to explain it fully, because explaining it would take forty-five minutes and require a diagram of my grandmother's kitchen, but I will tell you it involves a jar lid, a garden hose, and my Uncle Ronnie making a decision that still gets relitigated at every single Thanksgiving.

For years, Pickle Protocol lived only in our mouths. It was the thing we'd holler across a parking lot when someone was being unnecessarily stubborn. It was the password you had to know to prove you were really family and not just someone who married into it. It existed purely in the air, which felt fine, until my cousin Deanna decided it needed to be permanent.

Deanna is a person who commits. When she gets an idea, it does not stay an idea for long. So one spring she went looking for something carved out of actual wood -- not a printed thing, not a vinyl decal, not something that was going to peel off in the humidity -- and she found a little Kentucky workshop that does exactly that kind of hand-carved hardwood work. She wasn't looking for Pickle Protocol specifically, obviously. She was looking for the right kind of piece, the kind that feels solid and real and like it was made to last. She ended up buying what they call a carved resilience sign as a reference point, just to see the quality of the work, and she was sold on the craftsmanship immediately.

What she actually wanted was custom. And what she got, eventually, was a solid wood sign that says Pickle Protocol in big routed letters, hung in a place of honor in her kitchen above the coffee maker. It is, objectively, a beautiful object. The wood grain runs right through the lettering. It has weight to it. If you did not know my family, you would assume it was some kind of wellness mantra or a cooking term you had not heard of yet. Several of Deanna's coworkers have asked if it's a fermentation method.

Here is the thing about inside jokes made physical: they stop being just jokes. They become artifacts. That sign is going to outlast most of the furniture in Deanna's house. It is going to confuse her kids' friends for the next fifteen years. It will probably end up in an estate sale someday with a price tag that does not begin to capture what it meant to six people who were there for the original incident. That is not a sad thought, by the way. That is a beautiful one. Objects carry stories even when the stories aren't written on them.

This whole saga came back to me recently when I was poking around for a Mother's Day gift and landed on a mom wood sign that managed to say something true without being saccharine about it. Which is genuinely hard to do. Most sentimental gifts err so far on the side of sweet that they loop back around to meaning nothing. The good ones -- and a carved hardwood sign, when it's done right, can absolutely be one of the good ones -- say the thing plainly and then get out of the way and let the wood do the rest.

My point, if I have one, is this: the inside joke is actually the most honest form of communication a family has. It is shorthand for a whole shared history. And if you can get that shorthand carved into something that will sit in a kitchen for thirty years, you have given someone a gift that a greeting card cannot touch. Even if the only people who will ever understand it are the five of you who were there when Ronnie made his terrible decision with the garden hose. Especially then, maybe.
