Why Pine Gets the Stain and Poplar Gets the Paint

People are sometimes surprised that we only keep two kinds of board in the shop. We're Bluegrass Gifts, a small sign shop in Rineyville, Kentucky, and every sign we carve starts as either solid pine or solid poplar. Stained signs are pine. Painted signs are poplar. We don't switch them around, and there is a reason for that beyond habit.

Here is how we think about it. None of this is a secret, and it will help if you ever finish a board of your own.

Pine has grain worth showing

Pine is a softwood with a strong pattern. Each growth ring has a soft, pale band and a harder, darker band, and they take stain differently. The soft band drinks more of it, and the hard band sheds it. That contrast is what makes a stained pine board look like wood from across a room. Add a knot or two and you have a board with some character to it.

That same thirst is the catch. Pine can go blotchy under stain, especially around swirling grain, because different spots absorb at different rates. Woodworkers fight this in several ways, and pre-stain conditioner is the usual one. Even so, a little variation is part of the deal. No two pine boards finish exactly alike, and we think that is a point in their favor on a carved sign.

A note on names, because it comes up often: Dark Walnut, Western Oak and Golden Mahogany are stain colors. A Dark Walnut sign from our shop is pine wearing a walnut-colored stain. It isn't walnut, and we would rather tell you than let you guess.

Poplar wants to be painted

Poplar is technically a hardwood, though a fairly soft one, and its grain is fine and quiet. That is exactly what you want under paint. There is less pattern to telegraph through the finish, so the surface looks smooth and even.

Poplar under stain is another story. The wood often carries green or purplish mineral streaks, and a clear finish or a light stain leaves them in plain view. Paint hides them completely. So poplar's biggest weakness under stain simply doesn't matter under paint. Pair each board with the finish that suits it, and you get two good outcomes instead of two compromises. If you want to compare species for yourself, The Wood Database has good photos and working notes on both.

Why not plywood or MDF

We get asked this too, usually by people who have priced out a sheet of either. Both are cheaper per square foot, and both are the wrong material for what we do.

  • Plywood is thin layers glued together. Carve a V-groove into it and the cut runs down through those layers, so the walls of your letters show stripes of glue and face veneer.
  • MDF carves cleanly, but it is wood fiber and resin with no grain at all. It also doesn't care for moisture, and a swollen edge doesn't recover.

With a solid board, the walls of the letter are the same wood as the face, all the way down.

Where the contrast comes from

Whichever board it is, the carved letters on our signs stay bare wood. That is really the whole look. A V-carve cuts each letter with angled walls, and it goes deeper where a stroke is wider. So light falls on those walls differently than it does on the flat face, and the pale letter stands out from the dark finish around it. You aren't relying on a coat of paint in the letters to make them readable. The wood is doing the work.

The board's edges get the same finish as the face, so it reads as one solid piece from any angle rather than a dark top on a pale board.

What seals it

We seal our signs with polyurethane for indoor use. Outdoors is a different question. Sun and weather move wood more than any finish can fully stop, so if a sign is going outside, a spar urethane is the better choice, since it is made to flex with the wood. Even then, real wood outdoors will fade and shift over time. It helps to expect that going in.

If you are finishing a board at home

The short version is to choose the wood for the finish. If you want the grain to show, use pine and test your stain on a scrap from the same board first. If you want a clean, solid color, use poplar and let the paint hide its streaks. Most of the disappointing finishes we have seen came from asking a board to do something it was never going to be good at.
